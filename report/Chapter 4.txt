Methodology
In this study, we conducted a comprehensive comparison of three object detection algorithms: YOLO
(You Only Look Once), Faster R-CNN (Region-based Convolutional Neural Network), and GCN (Graph
Convolutional Network) with an adaptive deformation model. The objective was to evaluate their per-
formance in the context of text detection tasks.
Firstly, we implemented and trained the YOLO model, which excels in real-time detection. However,
we found that YOLO struggled to accurately capture multi-oriented and arbitrary-shaped text instances,
which are commonly encountered in text detection scenarios.
Subsequently, we explored the Faster R-CNN framework, known for its exceptional accuracy and ability
to handle complex objects. The two-stage architecture of Faster R-CNN, consisting of a region proposal
network (RPN) and a detection network, proved to be effective in capturing text instances with arbitrary
shapes and orientations. Faster R-CNN exhibited superior performance in localizing and recognizing
text, surpassing YOLO in this aspect.
To further enhance the detection capabilities, we incorporated a GCN with an adaptive deformation
model. This approach leveraged the graph structure of text instances, capturing the relationships and de-
formations within the text regions. The GCN’s ability to model dependencies among neighboring pixels
and exploit contextual information significantly improved the accuracy of text detection, particularly for
complex cases involving deformations and variations in text shape.
Through extensive experimentation and evaluation on diverse datasets, we compared the performance of
YOLO, Faster R-CNN, and GCN with adaptive deformation. The results demonstrated that the combina-
tion of Faster R-CNN and GCN with adaptive deformation achieved the highest accuracy and robustness
in text detection tasks, outperforming both YOLO and standalone Faster R-CNN models.
By conducting this comparative analysis, we provide valuable insights into the strengths and limita-
tions of YOLO, Faster R-CNN, and GCN for text detection. The findings highlight the importance of
considering the specific characteristics and challenges of text instances and the effectiveness of combin-
ing object detection algorithms with graph-based models to improve detection performance in complex
scenarios.

YOLO (You Only Look Once) is primarily designed for general object detection, it can also be applied to
text detection tasks with some modifications. In the context of text detection, YOLO is utilized to locate
and recognize textual content within images. Similar to its usage in object detection, YOLO divides the
input image into a grid and predicts bounding box coordinates and class probabilities for each grid cell.

To apply YOLO for text detection, the training data should be carefully prepared to include text instances
annotated with bounding boxes. This annotated dataset should cover various text sizes, orientations, and
styles that are representative of the target application. During training, the YOLO model is trained to
recognize and localize text regions by adjusting the network parameters through backpropagation and
gradient descent optimization.
When using YOLO for text detection, it is important to address some challenges specific to text. These

it suitable for applications where fast text detection is required, such as document analysis, OCR (Opti-
cal Character Recognition), or text extraction from images. However, it is important to note that while
YOLO can be effective for text detection, there may be alternative approaches specifically designed for
text-related tasks, such as algorithms that incorporate textual information or utilize graph-based repre-
sentations to capture the structural relationships between text elements.

4.2 Faster R-CNN (Region-based Convolutional Neural Network)
Faster R-CNN (Region-based Convolutional Neural Network) is a popular object detection framework
known for its exceptional accuracy in localizing and classifying objects. Unlike YOLO, which per-
forms detection in a single pass, Faster R-CNN adopts a two-stage approach. It consists of a region
proposal network (RPN) that generates potential object proposals and a detection network that refines
these proposals and assigns class labels. Below is a picture( 4.2 showing how FasterRCNN works:
Figure 4.2: Faster R-CNN Architecture Reprinted from the work of Shaoqing et al. (2016).[74]
When it comes to text detection, Faster R-CNN demonstrates distinct advantages over YOLO. Text in-
stances often exhibit variations in scale, aspect ratio, and orientation, making them more challenging to
detect accurately. Faster R-CNN’s two-stage architecture allows it to handle such complexities effec-
35
tively. The RPN generates region proposals that are better suited for capturing text regions with varying
scales and orientations. Moreover, the subsequent detection network refines these proposals and assigns
precise bounding box coordinates, leading to improved localization accuracy. The ability of Faster R-
CNN to adaptively adjust the region proposals to match the characteristics of text instances makes it
particularly well-suited for text detection tasks.
YOLO is known for its real-time detection capabilities, performing object detection in a single pass. It
divides the input image into a grid and predicts bounding box coordinates and class probabilities for each
grid cell. However, YOLO’s grid-based approach and fixed aspect ratios can limit its effectiveness in
capturing arbitrary-shaped and multi-oriented text instances. The fixed grid cells and aspect ratios may
not align well with the diverse shapes and orientations commonly found in the text, leading to challenges
in accurate localization and recognition.
On the other hand, Faster R-CNN addresses these limitations by adopting a two-stage approach. It
consists of a region proposal network (RPN) that generates potential object proposals and a subsequent
detection network that refines them and assigns class labels. This architecture allows Faster R-CNN to
adaptively adjust the region proposals based on the characteristics of the objects, including arbitrary-
shaped and multi-oriented text. The RPN can generate more flexible and precise proposals that align
with the varying shapes and orientations of text instances, leading to improved detection accuracy.
In the context of arbitrary-shaped and multi-oriented text detection, Faster R-CNN surpasses YOLO by
leveraging its two-stage architecture, which enables better handling of these complex text instances. The
region proposal stage in Faster R-CNN can generate more accurate proposals that align with the diverse
shapes and orientations of text, providing a strong foundation for subsequent detection and recognition.
As a result, Faster R-CNN is considered a better approach for capturing arbitrary-shaped and multi-
oriented text compared to YOLO.
It is worth noting that both YOLO and Faster R-CNN have their own strengths and weaknesses and
their suitability depends on the specific requirements of the text detection task. While YOLO excels
in real-time detection and overall object detection scenarios, Faster R-CNN’s ability to handle complex
text shapes and orientations makes it a preferred choice for tasks involving arbitrary-shaped and multi-
oriented text detection.
Despite its strengths, Faster R-CNN also has its limitations. One primary drawback is its computational
complexity. The two-stage process involving region proposal and detection networks can be computa-
tionally intensive, resulting in slower inference speeds compared to YOLO. Additionally, Faster R-CNN
may struggle with detecting small-sized text instances due to the limitations of anchor-based methods
in capturing fine-grained details. Furthermore, occlusions and overlapping text regions can pose chal-
lenges for Faster R-CNN, as the region proposal stage may struggle to generate accurate proposals in
such scenarios. Despite these limitations, Faster R-CNN’s superior accuracy and robustness in text de-
tection make it a favored choice in applications where precise localization and classification of text are
crucial.

The methodology of our approach, depicted in Figure 4.3, begins with the adoption of the ResNet-50

boundary proposals. Each proposal comprises N points, representing a potential text instance.
To refine the initial coarse proposals, we introduce an adaptive boundary deformation model. This
model performs iterative boundary deformations to obtain more accurate text boundaries, guided by
prior information such as the classification map, distance field, and direction field.
In summary, our methodology involves feature extraction using ResNet-50, multi-level feature fusion,
the boundary proposal model with multi-layer dilated convolutions, generation of coarse boundary pro-
posals using classification, distance, and direction fields, and iterative boundary deformation for refining
the proposals using prior information.

4.4.2 Dilated Convolutions
Dilated convolutions, also known as atrous convolutions, play a crucial role in improving text detection
methodologies. These convolutions introduce gaps or dilations between the values in the convolutional
kernel, which expands the receptive field of the model without increasing the number of parameters or
layers.
One of the key benefits of dilated convolutions in text detection is the increased receptive field. By
adjusting the dilation rate, the model can capture contextual information from a wider area surrounding
each pixel. This is particularly useful for detecting longer text instances or considering the context of
neighboring characters, leading to improved accuracy.
Another advantage of dilated convolutions is the preservation of spatial resolution. Unlike pooling or
down-sampling operations that reduce the resolution, dilated convolutions maintain high-resolution fea-
ture maps. This is crucial in text detection tasks where fine details and precise localization are essential
for accurate results.
Dilated convolutions excel at handling variable text sizes. In natural scenes, text instances can vary
significantly in size. Dilated convolutions adapt to different text scales without the need for multiple
layers or explicit resizing operations. This flexibility allows the model to effectively detect text instances
of various sizes within the same image.
Efficient modeling is another strength of dilated convolutions. They offer a computationally efficient
solution for capturing long-range dependencies and global context. By expanding the receptive field,
the model can leverage contextual information without significantly increasing the computational com-
plexity, resulting in improved efficiency and speed in text detection.
Overall, dilated convolutions enhance the ability of text detection models to capture contextual informa-
tion, handle varying text sizes, and preserve spatial resolution. These benefits contribute to improved
accuracy, robustness, and efficiency in detecting text instances in natural scenes.

4.4.1 Feature Pyramid Network (FPN)
The Feature Pyramid Network (FPN) is an architecture that addresses the challenge of detecting ob-
jects at different scales in computer vision tasks. It creates a pyramid-like structure of feature maps by
leveraging features from multiple levels of a convolutional neural network. The network combines a
top-down pathway for upsampling and a bottom-up pathway for downsampling. The fusion of feature
maps from different levels through lateral connections preserves fine-grained details and captures both
local and global context. In the context of text detection, FPN enables the model to handle varying sizes
of text instances and capture both fine details and overall context, enhancing accuracy and robustness.

4.4.2 Dilated Convolutions
Dilated convolutions, also known as atrous convolutions, play a crucial role in improving text detection
methodologies. These convolutions introduce gaps or dilations between the values in the convolutional
kernel, which expands the receptive field of the model without increasing the number of parameters or
layers.
One of the key benefits of dilated convolutions in text detection is the increased receptive field. By
adjusting the dilation rate, the model can capture contextual information from a wider area surrounding
each pixel. This is particularly useful for detecting longer text instances or considering the context of
neighboring characters, leading to improved accuracy.
Another advantage of dilated convolutions is the preservation of spatial resolution. Unlike pooling or
down-sampling operations that reduce the resolution, dilated convolutions maintain high-resolution fea-
ture maps. This is crucial in text detection tasks where fine details and precise localization are essential
for accurate results.
Dilated convolutions excel at handling variable text sizes. In natural scenes, text instances can vary
significantly in size. Dilated convolutions adapt to different text scales without the need for multiple
layers or explicit resizing operations. This flexibility allows the model to effectively detect text instances
of various sizes within the same image.
Efficient modeling is another strength of dilated convolutions. They offer a computationally efficient
solution for capturing long-range dependencies and global context. By expanding the receptive field,
the model can leverage contextual information without significantly increasing the computational com-
plexity, resulting in improved efficiency and speed in text detection.
Overall, dilated convolutions enhance the ability of text detection models to capture contextual informa-
tion, handle varying text sizes, and preserve spatial resolution. These benefits contribute to improved
accuracy, robustness, and efficiency in detecting text instances in natural scenes.

4.5 Adaptive Boundary Deformation
Motivated by interactive annotation techniques used in object segmentation and instance segmentation
methods, we propose a novel approach for arbitrary shape text detection. Our method involves deforming
a boundary proposal to accurately delineate the text boundary. Building upon the work of [81] and [82],
which model the sequence of vertices in an object’s polygon and treat control point locations as contin-
uous random variables, we introduce a Graph Neural Network that leverages image evidence to predict
per-vertex offsets pointing to the text boundary. Additionally, we draw inspiration from DeepSnake [83],
which employs circular convolutions with multi-layer 1-D convolutions for object segmentation. How-
ever, these existing methods primarily focus on individual topology or sequence context, which may not
be optimal for text detection. Existing methods in text detection often focus on either individual topol-
ogy context or sequence context, which is not fully satisfactory for accurate text detection. However,
considering the unique properties of texts, it is crucial to take into account both the topology context
42
and the sequence context simultaneously. Both of these contexts play significant roles in enhancing the
performance of text detection algorithms.
To facilitate batch processing, we uniformly sample N control points for each boundary proposal. These
control points form a closed polygon, capturing both the topology context and sequence context of
the proposal. To fully exploit these contexts, we introduce an adaptive boundary deformation model
combined with Graph Convolutional Networks (GCN) and Recurrent Neural Networks (RNN). This
model enables efficient feature learning and iterative boundary deformation, leading to the refinement
of coarse text boundaries.

4.5.1.1 Bilinear Interpolation
Bilinear interpolation is a technique used to estimate values within a grid based on known values at grid
points. It is commonly used in computer vision and image processing applications, including feature
extraction and image resizing.
In the context of the boundary refinement model, bilinear interpolation is employed to compute the
shared features (Fs) and prior features (Fp) for each control point. These features are obtained from a
CNN backbone and contain important information about the corresponding locations in the feature map.
The process of bilinear interpolation involves calculating a weighted average of the known values sur-
rounding the desired location. Specifically, for a given control point at coordinates (x, y), bilinear
interpolation considers the four nearest grid points in the feature map: (x1, y1), (x1, y2), (x2, y1), and
(x2, y2), where x1 ≤ x ≤ x2 and y1 ≤ y ≤ y2.
Let’s understand it with an example; Consider a set of data coordinates (xk, yk) that define the positions
of four points: Q11, Q21, Q12, and Q22. These points represent known values in the grid. Now, suppose
we have a new point P with coordinates (x, y) that lies between the xk and yk points. By applying bilinear
interpolation, we can calculate the value of point P based on the values of the surrounding points Q11,
Q21, Q12, and Q22 as shown in Figure 3.7. Bilinear interpolation takes into account the distances
43
between the known points and the target point P to determine an interpolated value that provides a
smooth and continuous estimation within the grid.

two linear interpolations along the x-axis, finding intermediate points R1 and R2 one linear interpolation

4.5.2.1 Graph Convolutional Network
Graph Convolutional Networks (GCNs) derive their ”convolutional” nature from the fact that they learn
filters during training, which are typically shared across all locations in the graph. This concept is similar
to how convolutional filters are learned in computer vision tasks, where filters are applied to image
inputs and shared across all pixel locations. Graph Convolutional Networks (GCNs) have emerged
as a powerful technique for analyzing graph-structured data. Unlike traditional convolutional neural
networks (CNNs) that operate on regular grid-like data, GCNs can handle irregular and non-grid data
represented as graphs as shown in Figure 4.8.

elements. Formally, a graph G is defined as G = (V, E), where V represents the set of vertices/nodes, and
E represents the set of edges. In the context of GCNs, we define a graph G = (V, E), where each node i is
associated with initial node features xi. The input to the GCN model consists of the graph itself and the
initial node features X, which is a matrix of size N × D, where N represents the number of nodes and D
represents the dimensionality of the node features. The goal of the GCN is to learn representations for
each node, and at the final layer, we obtain node representations denoted as Z, which is a matrix of size

4.5 Adaptive Boundary Deformation
Motivated by interactive annotation techniques used in object segmentation and instance segmentation
methods, we propose a novel approach for arbitrary shape text detection. Our method involves deforming
a boundary proposal to accurately delineate the text boundary. Building upon the work of [81] and [82],
which model the sequence of vertices in an object’s polygon and treat control point locations as contin-
uous random variables, we introduce a Graph Neural Network that leverages image evidence to predict
per-vertex offsets pointing to the text boundary. Additionally, we draw inspiration from DeepSnake [83],
which employs circular convolutions with multi-layer 1-D convolutions for object segmentation. How-
ever, these existing methods primarily focus on individual topology or sequence context, which may not
be optimal for text detection. Existing methods in text detection often focus on either individual topol-
ogy context or sequence context, which is not fully satisfactory for accurate text detection. However,
considering the unique properties of texts, it is crucial to take into account both the topology context
42
and the sequence context simultaneously. Both of these contexts play significant roles in enhancing the
performance of text detection algorithms.
To facilitate batch processing, we uniformly sample N control points for each boundary proposal. These
control points form a closed polygon, capturing both the topology context and sequence context of
the proposal. To fully exploit these contexts, we introduce an adaptive boundary deformation model
combined with Graph Convolutional Networks (GCN) and Recurrent Neural Networks (RNN). This
model enables efficient feature learning and iterative boundary deformation, leading to the refinement
of coarse text boundaries.

4.5.1.1 Bilinear Interpolation
Bilinear interpolation is a technique used to estimate values within a grid based on known values at grid
points. It is commonly used in computer vision and image processing applications, including feature
extraction and image resizing.
In the context of the boundary refinement model, bilinear interpolation is employed to compute the
shared features (Fs) and prior features (Fp) for each control point. These features are obtained from a
CNN backbone and contain important information about the corresponding locations in the feature map.
The process of bilinear interpolation involves calculating a weighted average of the known values sur-
rounding the desired location. Specifically, for a given control point at coordinates (x, y), bilinear
interpolation considers the four nearest grid points in the feature map: (x1, y1), (x1, y2), (x2, y1), and
(x2, y2), where x1 ≤ x ≤ x2 and y1 ≤ y ≤ y2.
Let’s understand it with an example; Consider a set of data coordinates (xk, yk) that define the positions
of four points: Q11, Q21, Q12, and Q22. These points represent known values in the grid. Now, suppose
we have a new point P with coordinates (x, y) that lies between the xk and yk points. By applying bilinear
interpolation, we can calculate the value of point P based on the values of the surrounding points Q11,
Q21, Q12, and Q22 as shown in Figure 3.7. Bilinear interpolation takes into account the distances

between the known points and the target point P to determine an interpolated value that provides a
smooth and continuous estimation within the grid.

to how convolutional filters are learned in computer vision tasks, where filters are applied to image
inputs and shared across all pixel locations. Graph Convolutional Networks (GCNs) have emerged
as a powerful technique for analyzing graph-structured data. Unlike traditional convolutional neural
networks (CNNs) that operate on regular grid-like data, GCNs can handle irregular and non-grid data
represented as graphs as shown in Figure 4.8.

A graph is a data structure that consists of a set of vertices (also known as nodes) and a set of edges con-

elements. Formally, a graph G is defined as G = (V, E), where V represents the set of vertices/nodes, and
E represents the set of edges. In the context of GCNs, we define a graph G = (V, E), where each node i is

Bi-LSTM
A Bidirectional Long Short-Term Memory (Bi-LSTM) is a type of recurrent neural network (RNN) that
processes sequential data in both forward and backward directions. It is an extension of the traditional
LSTM architecture, which is designed to capture long-term dependencies and handle sequential data.
The Bi-LSTM consists of two LSTM layers, one processing the input sequence in the forward direction
and the other in the backward direction. The forward LSTM layer processes the sequence from the
beginning to the end, while the backward LSTM layer processes the sequence in reverse order, starting
from the last element. By capturing information from both directions, the Bi-LSTM is able to effectively
model the dependencies in the sequence.
At each time step, the forward and backward LSTM layers receive the input from the previous time step,
as well as their respective hidden states. The hidden states of the forward LSTM layer capture the infor-
mation from the past, while the hidden states of the backward LSTM layer capture the information from
the future. The outputs of the forward and backward LSTM layers at each time step are concatenated to
obtain the final output of the Bi-LSTM.
The Bi-LSTM architecture allows the model to capture both local and global dependencies in the input
sequence. The forward pass of the Bi-LSTM processes the sequence and generates hidden representa-
tions that capture the dependencies up to the current time step, while the backward pass processes the
sequence in reverse and captures the dependencies from the future. By combining these representations,
the Bi-LSTM can make more informed predictions and better understand the context of the sequential
data.
In natural language processing tasks, the Bi-LSTM is often used for tasks such as sentiment analysis,
named entity recognition, and machine translation, where understanding the context of the words in a
sentence or sequence is crucial. The bidirectional nature of the Bi-LSTM allows it to effectively capture
dependencies in both directions, improving the model’s ability to capture long-term dependencies and
make accurate predictions.

4.6 Implementation Details
In our experimental setup, the initial phase involves pre-training the network on SynthText using a single
epoch. During this pre-training, images are subjected to random cropping and resizing, resulting in a
uniform size of 512 × 512. The training process utilizes the Adam optimizer with a fixed learning rate
of 0.001 and mini-batches containing 16 samples.
For fine-tuning, we proceed by randomly cropping the text regions from the images and resizing them
to dimensions of 640 × 640. This stage employs a mini-batch size of 12 and continues to use the Adam
optimizer. The initial learning rate of 0.001 is subsequently decreased to 0.9 of its original value after
every 50 epochs. Data augmentation techniques, including random rotation within the range of -60° to
60° (sampled from a Gaussian distribution), random cropping, and random flipping, contribute to the
robustness of the model.
In the inference phase, the aspect ratio of test images is maintained, and they are then resized and padded
to a consistent size for testing purposes. Our implementation employs PyTorch 1.7 and Python 3, and
the training is executed on a single RTX-3090 GPU, while testing occurs on a single GeForce RTX-2080
GPU, complemented by an Intel Xeon Silver 4108 CPU @ 1.80GHz.
